Why This Plan Exists

The To-Do Prioritizer was built on assumptions: that users struggle with prioritization, that a scoring model helps, and that showing ranked tasks increases completion. These are reasonable hypotheses — but a PM who ships without validating assumptions is gambling with engineering time.

This research plan shows how I'd validate (or invalidate) those assumptions before committing to features like Focus Mode or the experiments in the metrics write-up.

What this demonstrates: Research planning, screener design, interview guide writing, survey methodology, analysis frameworks, and the ability to connect research findings to product decisions.

1. Research Objectives

Every research effort needs clear questions it's trying to answer. Without them, you end up with interesting data that doesn't inform any decision.

Primary Research Questions

#QuestionWhy It MattersDecision It Informs
RQ1 How do people currently decide what to work on first when they have multiple tasks? If people already have effective systems, a prioritization tool solves a problem that doesn't exist Whether the core product concept is viable
RQ2 At what point does a task list become overwhelming? Is there a threshold? Determines when our product adds value — users with 3 tasks don't need prioritization help Focus Mode trigger point (currently assumed at 10+ tasks)
RQ3 What factors do people weigh when deciding task priority, and do they do it consistently? Validates our scoring dimensions (urgency, importance, effort) and whether users would trust an automated score Scoring model weights and which dimensions to include
RQ4 What does "completing a task" feel like? Is there a meaningful reward signal? If completion isn't satisfying, our North Star metric (Weekly Task Completion Rate) may be the wrong thing to optimize Gamification and motivation features
RQ5 What tools are people using today, and what's missing? Competitive landscape from the user's perspective — not what competitors offer, but what users actually use and wish was different Positioning and feature differentiation

Assumptions to Validate

Critical assumption: These are beliefs baked into the product that would change our roadmap if proven wrong. I'd explicitly test each one.
AssumptionRisk If WrongHow We'd Test
Users want an algorithm to rank their tasks High — core value prop is invalid Interview: Ask about trust in automated prioritization. Survey: Likert scale on "I'd trust a tool to rank my tasks"
Urgency, importance, and effort are the right scoring dimensions Medium — scoring model produces irrelevant rankings Interview: Ask users to think aloud while prioritizing real tasks. Note which factors they mention unprompted
Seeing fewer tasks (Focus Mode) reduces overwhelm Medium — Focus Mode feature investment is wasted Interview: Show a 15-task list vs. a 3-task list and ask which feels more actionable. Observe emotional response
Task completion is the right success metric (not time management or stress reduction) High — we'd be optimizing the wrong outcome Interview: Ask "What would make you feel like a task management tool was actually helping you?"

2. Participant Recruitment

Who you talk to matters as much as what you ask. The screener below is designed to find people who actually experience the problem we're solving — not just anyone who uses a to-do list.

Target Participant Profile

  • Who: Knowledge workers (IC or manager) who manage personal task lists for work
  • Volume: Manages 10+ tasks per week across at least 2 projects or workstreams
  • Tool use: Currently uses at least one digital task tool (Todoist, Notion, Asana, Apple Reminders, etc.)
  • Pain: Has experienced overwhelm or difficulty deciding what to work on first
  • Exclusions: Professional project managers (different workflow), people who only use paper lists (different mental model)

Participant Mix Goals

Diversity in the participant pool prevents confirmation bias. I'd aim for:

  • At least 3 participants who use a structured tool (Todoist, Asana, TickTick)
  • At least 2 who use an unstructured tool (Apple Notes, Notion free-form, sticky notes + digital)
  • At least 2 managers and at least 3 individual contributors
  • At least 1 participant who has tried and abandoned a task management tool

3. Interview Guide

30-minute semi-structured interviews. The guide below follows a deliberate arc: start broad (current behavior), go deep (pain points and mental models), then get specific (reactions to concepts). I never ask leading questions or pitch the product.

Interviewing principle: Every question includes the intent (what I'm trying to learn) so a teammate could run these interviews and know what to probe on. Good research plans are runnable by anyone, not just the person who wrote them.

Part 1: Current Behavior (10 min)

Goal: Understand how they manage tasks today, without introducing our product concepts.

Question 1
"Walk me through how you decided what to work on first this morning."
Intent: Surface their actual decision-making process, not their idealized one. Morning routines reveal real habits.
  • "What made you pick that task over the others?"
  • "Was there anything you considered working on but didn't? Why not?"
Question 2
"Can you show me your current task list or system? Just whatever you have open right now."
Intent: See the actual artifact, not a description of it. Observe how many tasks, how they're organized, what's overdue.
  • "How long have some of these been on the list?"
  • "Are there any tasks here you know you'll never actually do?"
Question 3
"Tell me about a time recently when you had too many things to do and had to choose. What happened?"
Intent: Get a specific story, not a generalization. Stories reveal emotions, stakes, and real consequences.
  • "How did that turn out?"
  • "Looking back, would you have prioritized differently?"

Part 2: Pain Points & Mental Models (12 min)

Goal: Understand where their current system breaks down and how they think about priority.

Question 4
"When you say a task is 'important,' what does that mean to you? What makes one task more important than another?"
Intent: Validate whether our scoring dimensions (urgency, importance, effort) match how users actually think. Listen for factors we haven't considered.
  • "Is 'important' different from 'urgent' in your mind? How?"
  • "Does the size of a task affect whether you do it first or put it off?"
Question 5
"What's the worst thing that happens when you prioritize wrong? Has that actually happened?"
Intent: Understand the stakes. If mis-prioritization has no real consequences, the pain isn't strong enough to drive adoption.
  • "How did you find out you'd prioritized the wrong thing?"
  • "What did you do about it?"
Question 6
"Have you ever tried a tool or system that was supposed to help with prioritization? What happened?"
Intent: Understand competitive landscape from user's POV. Learn what features they valued and why they stayed or left.
  • "What did you like about it?"
  • "Why did you stop using it?" (if applicable)
  • "What would have made you keep using it?"

Part 3: Concept Reactions (8 min)

Goal: Test specific product concepts without leading. Show, don't tell.

Question 7
"Imagine a tool that looked at your task list and ranked everything by a score based on urgency, importance, and effort. How would you feel about that?"
Intent: Test trust in automated prioritization. Watch for enthusiasm vs. skepticism. Don't oversell — present it neutrally.
  • "Would you trust the ranking? What would make you trust it more or less?"
  • "Would you want to adjust the weights, or would you prefer it just worked?"
Question 8
[Show two screenshots: full 15-task list vs. Focus Mode with top 3] "Which of these feels more useful to look at first thing in the morning? Why?"
Intent: Validate the Focus Mode hypothesis. Observe emotional reaction — relief? Anxiety about hidden tasks? Curiosity?
  • "Would you worry about the tasks you can't see?"
  • "How many tasks would you want to see in this focused view?"
Question 9
"If a tool like this existed, what's the one thing it would need to do for you to actually use it every day?"
Intent: Identify the single most important feature from the user's perspective. Often reveals something we haven't thought of.

4. Validation Survey

After interviews, I'd run a short quantitative survey to test whether patterns from 8-10 interviews hold across a broader sample. The survey is intentionally short (3 minutes) to maximize completion rate.

Survey Structure

#QuestionTypeMaps to RQ
S1 How many tasks are you currently tracking right now? Multiple choice (1-5, 6-10, 11-20, 20+) RQ2 (overwhelm threshold)
S2 How often do you feel unsure about what to work on next? 5-point Likert (Never → Always) RQ1 (problem validation)
S3 When you have too many tasks, what do you do first? Rank order: easiest first / most urgent / most important / smallest / random RQ3 (prioritization factors)
S4 "I would trust a tool to automatically rank my tasks by priority" 5-point Likert (Strongly disagree → Strongly agree) Assumption: users want automated ranking
S5 Which factors should a prioritization tool consider? (Select all that apply) Multi-select: urgency, importance, effort, deadline, dependencies, energy level, other RQ3 (scoring dimensions)
S6 "Seeing only my top 3 tasks would help me focus better" 5-point Likert (Strongly disagree → Strongly agree) Assumption: Focus Mode reduces overwhelm
S7 What's the #1 thing that's broken about how you manage tasks today? Open text (optional) RQ1 (open-ended pain discovery)

Distribution Plan

  • Platform: Google Forms (free, good enough for this sample size)
  • Target: 50+ complete responses
  • Channels: Same communities as interview recruitment, plus Twitter/X and relevant Slack communities
  • Incentive: Enter to win a $25 gift card (optional — increases response rate by ~20% in my experience)

5. Analysis Framework

Raw research data is useless without a structured way to turn it into decisions. Here's how I'd analyze and synthesize findings.

Interview Analysis: Affinity Mapping

After each interview, I'd create atomic notes (one insight per sticky note) and group them into themes. Here's what the affinity map categories might look like:

CategoryWhat Goes HereExample Insight
Current behavior How people manage tasks today, tools they use, habits "P3 checks Notion first thing but admits she often just does whatever feels urgent"
Decision factors What people consider when prioritizing (maps to scoring model) "5 of 8 participants mentioned 'deadline' as a factor, but only 2 mentioned 'effort'"
Pain points Where current systems break down "P6: 'I know everything on my list is important, that's the problem'"
Emotional response How overwhelm feels, what completion feels like "P1 described anxiety looking at a full list — 'it's like a wall of obligations'"
Concept reactions Responses to automated ranking and Focus Mode concepts "P4 was skeptical of auto-ranking but loved the focused view: 'just tell me my top 3'"
Surprises Anything unexpected that challenges our assumptions "3 participants said they deliberately keep zombie tasks to feel productive when they complete easy wins"

Survey Analysis

  • Likert scales: Report median and distribution, not just averages. A bimodal distribution (people either love or hate auto-ranking) is more actionable than a neutral average.
  • Rank order (S3): Use weighted ranking to determine dominant prioritization strategy across the sample.
  • Multi-select (S5): Frequency analysis to validate or challenge our 3-dimension scoring model.
  • Open text (S7): Thematic coding, looking for patterns that match or contradict interview findings.

Sample Synthesis Output

Here's what a key finding might look like after synthesis. This is the format I'd use to present to stakeholders:

Finding: Users want guidance, not automation

7 of 8 participants expressed interest in seeing their tasks ranked, but 5 of 8 wanted to understand why a task was ranked high — and the ability to override the ranking. Blind trust in an algorithm was low.

"I'd want to see the score, but I'd also want to drag things around. Sometimes I know something is urgent that the tool doesn't." — P3
"If it just told me what to do with no explanation, I'd feel like I'm not in control." — P7

Product implication: The scoring model should be visible and adjustable, not hidden. The P2 "Why these 3?" tooltip in the Focus Mode spec should be elevated to P0. Consider adding drag-to-reorder within Focus Mode.

Finding: The overwhelm threshold is lower than assumed

We assumed overwhelm kicks in at 10+ tasks. Interview data suggests it starts at 6-8 tasks for most participants, especially when tasks span multiple projects.

"Even 7 things feels like a lot when they're all for different clients." — P5

Product implication: Consider showing Focus Mode as default for users with 7+ tasks, not 10+. Adjust the experiment design in the metrics write-up to test this lower threshold.

6. From Research to Product Decisions

Research is only valuable if it changes what you build. Here's how each research question maps to a concrete product decision.

Research QuestionIf ValidatedIf Invalidated
RQ1: Do people struggle to decide what to work on first? Proceed with current product direction. The core value prop is sound. Pivot: Maybe the problem isn't prioritization but tracking, delegation, or time-boxing. Revisit the entire product concept.
RQ2: Is there an overwhelm threshold? Use the threshold to determine when to trigger Focus Mode and onboarding prompts. Focus Mode may not be the right intervention. Explore progressive disclosure or filtering instead.
RQ3: Are urgency, importance, effort the right dimensions? Keep current scoring model. May adjust weights based on how users rank factors. Add missing dimensions (deadline, energy level, dependencies) or simplify to fewer factors.
RQ4: Is task completion the right success metric? Keep Weekly Task Completion Rate as North Star. Invest in completion celebrations. Explore alternative North Stars: stress reduction, time saved, or "felt productive today" self-report.
RQ5: What's missing from current tools? Build the differentiated feature that users can't find elsewhere. If nothing is missing, our positioning needs to be about doing existing things better, not adding new capabilities.

7. Research Timeline

WeekActivityDeliverable
Week 1 Recruit participants, finalize screener, pilot interview guide with 1 participant Confirmed participant list (8-10), revised interview guide
Week 2 Conduct 8-10 interviews (2-3 per day), take atomic notes after each session Interview notes, initial affinity map, survey launched mid-week
Week 3 Close survey, complete affinity mapping, synthesize findings, write report Research report with findings, product implications, and recommended next steps
Why 3 weeks: Research shouldn't block product development indefinitely. Three weeks is enough to get directional confidence on critical assumptions without becoming a bottleneck. If we need deeper validation on a specific finding, we can run a focused follow-up study.
