Case Study 1: Growing Credit Karma’s Performance Advertising from Zero to $120K/Month

Role
Customer Success Manager
Company
Criteo
Timeline
2023–2026
Outcome
$0 → $120K/month

Background

Credit Karma is a consumer fintech platform serving millions of users across credit monitoring, personal finance, and financial product recommendations. As a Criteo client, they had access to one of the largest commerce media networks — but they weren’t using it.

When I took over the account, Credit Karma had a minimal footprint on the Criteo platform. Budget was at or near baseline. Engagement was reactive, not strategic. The account was at risk of churning not from dissatisfaction, but from irrelevance.

My framing: My job, formally, was retention. But I treated it like a product problem: why isn’t this client getting value, and what would need to be true for them to invest more?

The Problem

Credit Karma’s category — financial services — is one of the more complex for performance advertising. Unlike e-commerce clients with clear conversion events (purchases), fintech advertisers measure value through softer signals: credit card applications, loan inquiries, sign-ups. Their attribution models were harder to defend, and their internal stakeholders were skeptical of ad spend ROI.

The result was a chicken-and-egg situation: they weren’t spending enough to generate meaningful data, which meant the platform’s optimization algorithms couldn’t perform, which gave them no reason to increase spend. They were stuck.

Root Causes I Identified

#Root CauseImpact
1 Misaligned success metrics — Credit Karma’s internal KPIs didn’t map cleanly to what Criteo optimized for out of the box They were evaluating performance through a lens the platform wasn’t calibrated to
2 Lack of confidence in attribution — Their team wasn’t convinced they could accurately credit Criteo for downstream conversions Budget justification was difficult internally
3 No test-and-learn structure — Without a deliberate experimentation framework, there was no way to generate evidence No data to support investment growth

Discovery & Research

I ran a structured discovery process with their team over several weeks:

  • Stakeholder interviews with their media buyers and analytics team to understand how they evaluated campaign success and what internal approval looked like for budget increases
  • Platform data analysis — pulled historical campaign performance data in Looker to identify signals of value that hadn’t been surfaced or framed correctly
  • Competitive benchmarking — reviewed how comparable fintech advertisers were structuring campaigns and what attribution approaches were working
  • Funnel mapping — walked through their full customer journey to identify which conversion events were trackable, attributable, and meaningful enough to use as optimization signals
  • Shared dashboard utilization — gave Credit Karma’s team direct visibility into live campaign metrics, creative performance, and KPI analytics to reduce reporting lag and build internal confidence

Hypothesis

If we reframe Credit Karma’s campaign structure around a conversion event they could confidently attribute — even a softer one, like a qualified click-through to a financial product page — and pair that with a structured 60-day test at a committed spend level, then the platform’s algorithms would have enough signal to optimize, performance would improve, and their internal stakeholders would have the data they needed to approve larger budgets.
“The unlock wasn’t the product — it was the framework for proving the product’s value.”

Strategy & Actions

1. Rebuilt the measurement model
Working cross-functionally with Criteo’s data and ad ops teams, I helped Credit Karma establish a revised attribution framework that used a conversion event they could both track reliably and defend internally. This required negotiating what “success” looked like before a single dollar was spent — essentially a lightweight discovery-to-alignment process.
2. Proposed a phased investment structure
Rather than asking them to increase budget speculatively, I structured a 60-day test at a defined spend level with clear go/no-go criteria built in. This gave their internal stakeholders a finite commitment and a specific decision point. It also gave the platform time to accumulate signal.
3. Ran regular performance reviews as product reviews
I shifted our monthly check-ins from status updates to structured learning sessions: what did we test, what did we observe, what does that tell us, what do we do next. I anchored every session in the shared client dashboard — walking through live campaign KPIs, creative performance data, and attribution trends together — so their team wasn’t receiving a report, they were developing a point of view. This built their internal fluency and made it significantly easier for them to advocate for budget increases with their own leadership.
4. Surfaced and escalated product gaps
During this process, I identified workflow inefficiencies and UI issues creating confusion across client accounts. One example: the platform was displaying audience segments under 5,000 users as “0” — technically accurate but deeply misleading. I documented the issue with client impact context and a clear proposed solution, and advocated for the fix with our Product team. The result was a UI update that surfaced the real audience size with a contextual warning. It was a small change with an outsized effect on client confidence.
PM insight: The worst UX isn’t always a broken feature — it’s a misleading one. A number showing “0” when the real answer is “4,800” doesn’t just create confusion; it destroys trust in the entire product.

What I Learned

“The first product question isn’t ‘what does this client need to configure?’ — it’s ‘what does this client need to believe?’”

The biggest lesson was that the product’s value wasn’t the blocker — the client’s confidence in measuring that value was. Until we fixed the measurement model, no amount of optimization or platform capability mattered.

This also reinforced for me that post-sale customer relationships are fundamentally a product discovery exercise. Every conversation is a user interview. Every escalation is a signal. The clients who struggle aren’t telling you they hate the product — they’re telling you the product isn’t solving the right problem in the right way.

Case Study 2: Identifying a Platform Gap for Non-Traditional Verticals

Role
TSS & CSM
Company
Criteo
Type
Product Discovery & Feedback
Outcome
Systemic platform fix

Background

Criteo’s platform was built on the foundation of e-commerce. Its core dynamic advertising product — serving personalized banners based on a user’s browsing and purchase history — was designed around a straightforward premise: retailers have product catalogs, products have IDs, and those IDs power the dynamic creative.

That assumption works seamlessly for a fashion retailer or electronics brand. It starts to break down the moment you move outside of traditional e-commerce — into financial services, travel, entertainment, B2B software, or any vertical where the concept of a “product” doesn’t map neatly onto a SKU in a feed.

The Problem

Non-traditional clients who wanted to run dynamic banners — one of Criteo’s most effective ad formats — kept hitting the same wall. The platform required product IDs in the creative feed to power dynamic content. For e-commerce clients, those IDs are variable: they’re pulled dynamically from the feed based on what a user has browsed or purchased.

For non-traditional clients, that data structure simply didn’t exist. The workaround: ask clients to hard-code their product IDs — essentially locking their dynamic banners to a fixed set of static values instead of truly dynamic, personalized content.

The core issue: A dynamic ad format that wasn’t actually dynamic. Clients were getting a technically functional implementation that fell well short of the product’s value proposition. This wasn’t a one-off configuration issue — it was a systemic gap affecting an entire and growing segment of the client base.

Discovery & Research

The problem surfaced gradually through client-facing work rather than a formal audit:

  • Repeated escalations with the same root cause — multiple non-traditional clients across financial services, travel, and other verticals were independently hitting the same technical wall during implementation. The consistency of the pattern was the first signal.
  • Workaround fatigue — the hard-coded product ID solution was generating downstream issues: creatives that couldn’t adapt to user behavior, implementation timelines that stretched unnecessarily long, and internal confusion about whether clients were getting the product they’d been sold.
  • Client feedback synthesis — conversations revealed a gap between expectation and reality. Clients had purchased dynamic advertising based on its personalization promise, and the workaround was delivering something closer to static.
  • Cross-functional pattern recognition — by working closely with both the technical implementation side and the client-facing success side, I was positioned to connect dots that might not have been visible from either role alone.

Hypothesis

The platform’s reliance on hard-coded product IDs for non-traditional verticals wasn’t a client problem — it was a product architecture assumption that had outlived its original scope. If the platform could support variable product ID structures that accommodated non-catalog-based verticals, we could eliminate a major onboarding blocker, improve creative performance for an entire client segment, and extend the true value of dynamic advertising beyond e-commerce.

“The fix wasn’t about building something new. It was about making what already existed work for a broader definition of ‘product.’”

What I Did

Documented the pattern, not just the instance
Rather than escalating individual client tickets, I reframed the issue at the pattern level — documenting the range of non-traditional verticals affected, the frequency of the workaround, the client impact, and the downstream consequences for both onboarding speed and creative performance. This gave the Product and Engineering teams the scope they needed to evaluate the issue as a platform investment, not a one-off fix.
Proposed a clear solution with business context
I advocated for updating the system to support variable product IDs alongside the existing hard-coded approach — preserving backward compatibility for e-commerce clients while removing the structural barrier for non-traditional ones. I framed the business case around client segment expansion: the non-traditional vertical opportunity was real and growing, and this gap was a direct obstacle to capturing it.
Collaborated across technical and product stakeholders
Bridging the gap between what clients were experiencing and what engineering needed to act on required translating client-facing friction into technical specifics — and technical constraints back into client-facing language. I worked with internal stakeholders to ensure the feedback landed with the right context on both sides.

Outcome

Surfacing this issue shifted how our team approached non-traditional vertical onboarding. The hard-coded workaround went from an undocumented tribal knowledge fix to a flagged platform limitation with a clear engineering path forward — which changed the conversation both internally and with clients.

  • Engineering solution reduced onboarding friction for non-traditional verticals
  • Expanded the platform’s addressable client base beyond e-commerce
  • Established a pattern for how customer-facing teams could surface systemic product issues

What I Learned

“Any individual escalation looks like a client problem. Seeing the same issue surface across five different verticals in six months tells you something different: the product has an assumption baked in that no longer holds.”

The most important skill in this work wasn’t technical — it was pattern recognition across clients. This experience shaped how I think about the relationship between customer-facing roles and product development.

The people closest to clients don’t just resolve issues — they generate the highest-signal product intelligence in the company, if they know how to look for patterns and communicate them in a way that the product organization can act on. That’s the bridge I want to keep building.
